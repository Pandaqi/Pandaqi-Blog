_In an attempt to play more games as a game developer and learn from them, I've started trying out all sorts of games. This week I've tried "Two Point Museum". Below are my quick thoughts on it!_

I played Two Point Hospital a long time ago. I thought it was kinda neat, but after creating a few (somewhat) functional hospitals I never played it again. I did keep up with the Two Point releases though, because they were doing something right.

They created management games that looked colorful and playful, that were simple enough to start playing quickly, that had interesting themes I could imagine myself enjoying.

Then _Two Point Museum_ released, everyone raved about how amazing it was, and I told myself I should play it. 

Now the time had finally come!

I was ready to love the game, to be just as extatic about it, but ... I wasn't.

Why not? At first, I wasn't quite sure.

As I played my first game, I kept thinking "oh that's neat" and "oh that's a great game mechanic" and "yes that's a clever way to do it". In fact, that same evening, I wrote down a small game idea that amounted to taking Two Point Museum's _Expedition_ mechanic and turning it into its own game.

At the same time, I wasn't really having fun. I was just going through the motions. There were no real surprises here, nothing to make me lean forward, and I felt a bit too much friction with all the game elements. I tried to make everything look pretty and flow nicely, which was really just a default subconscious decision as a designer/illustrator myself, but that too felt less fun than doing it in other games. 

{{% remark %}}
I spent hours and hours designing extremely well-themed parks in Parkitect ... and then I finally actually opened the park :p
{{% /remark %}}

It seems like these management(-lite) games can be played in two ways. You can either ignore the aesthetic, ignore everything that's not about core gameplay, and try to beat the game as quickly as possible. Or you can take the time to make your museum look pretty, to add decorations, to really design something lovely and functioning.

**And I think the reason I didn't really enjoy _Two Point Museum_ is because it _tries to be both_ and thus _fails at being either of them_.**

There's this big system around going on expeditions and managing employees. Quite early on in the game, I already had to build some special room and remember to send employees there to train a very specific skill they needed to have. My nice museum decorating rhythm kept being interrupted by the thought "oh I need to send people on some new expedition!".

These systems are _fine_ and sometimes even _pretty interesting_. But they are systems that belong to a more involved and more brain-burning game. They are systems that _suggest_ a really tough, strategic, hard-to-beat management game. Unfortuantely, they don't follow through on this, because the rest of the game is extremely easy and forgiving and without many mechanics at all. 

You have to actively destroy yourself to not make a profit. You can pretty much buy anything and place it anywhere and it will all work out. Guests move around very freely and are exceptionally ready to donate and love your exhibits. There are some _hints_ of mechanics, such as certain frozen artefacts needing to be placed in a cold room, but it doesn't go beyond that. So now we have a game that _suggests_ this extremely cozy, laid-back, simple, easy-to-beat management game. 

These two sides of the game could have combined into something really interesting. Instead, they do not mix well---or so I felt while playing---and break each other down.

Let me give a specific example. A large part of such a management/building game is buying the things you need and placing them where you need them. Except in this game, you only get new stuff to display after going on expeditions. Furthermore, you don't know _what_ you will get, or if the expedition succeeds at all. Now we have two mechanics at war with each other.

* I literally can't expand my museum because I have nothing more to place.
* But I can't _prepare_ for anything, or _plan/design_ for it, because I have no clue what I will get and when I will get it.

This amounts to lots of waiting around. This amounts to getting something, shrugging, dropping it in the first empty spot that's been waiting for a month, and then being back to status quo again. Even if you try to "maximize" the exhibit's value, and decorate it seriously, it's not a challenge or a nice way to waste time. You plonk down the three things it says it needs and you're done. No puzzle, no strategy, no way to bridge the time gap between expeditions.

Again, this _could_ have been really interesting. The game could have had this really nice flow of not knowing what you'll get, surprising results, needing to have contingency plans, etcetera. Instead the two sides never really combine into one nice gameplay loop.

_Could I relax and just design a nice museum?_ No, because I had to constantly engage with the more involved mechanics (around employees, expeditions, new stuff introduced, etcetera).

_Could I challenge myself, play seriously, use strategy and clever play to feel engaged?_ No, because none of the mechanics are actually that deep or interesting, especially not long-term. The game throws some obstacles your way, but they're either always solved easily in exactly the same way, or the obstacle is _randomness_ or _you just need to wait a few months_---which is not engaging for long.

After playing for a handful of hours, I never felt the need to pick it up again. There wasn't enough to engage or satisfy me, but there was too much friction to use it as a low energy casual game. Which makes me a bit sad, if you couldn't already tell from my writing. The game looks great, the ideas are great, their mechanics make a lot of sense ... but it just did not click for me. 

{{% remark %}}
As a game developer, I _know_ how hard it is to design games and how much effort goes into it. I appreciate even the tiny things like animated UI or Quality Of Life features. That's why it makes me sad to see all that work not amount to something I enjoy or the potential it had.
{{% /remark %}}

I always try to be constructive, so let's finish by asking myself: how would I change the design? What would I do differently?

In this case, I would design each system to be deeper and more involved. Give the expedition aspect of the game a load of extra depth and options and gameplay impact. **But,** I would lock all those deep and interesting systems behind a toggle. You don't want to deal with it? Turn it off or set it to "automatic". The game will just run automatic expeditions in the background and you get something back once in a while. No need to deal with all that if you want to just design a cozy museum and relax.

Now the game feels like two halves of different games that don't mix.

Those were my thoughts,
