_In an attempt to play more games as a game developer and learn from them, I've started trying out all sorts of games. This week I've tried "The Fertile Crescent (2)". Below are my quick thoughts on it!_

One of my first video games was Age of Empires. Yes, part 1, the original. It felt like magic to my 7-year-old self, playing it on our massive ancient computer, and I remember being frustrated that I could never really beat the tougher computer opponents.

Well, that trauma seems to be never-ending, because over the years I reinstalled the old Age of Empires games many times, and I _still_ can't beat the hardest opponents ;) I'm much older now, and wiser, and have all this strategy in my backpocket, but somehow I just wasn't having fun and felt like every game was the same. I would rapidly click all sorts of random upgrades. I wasn't going to read a wikipedia and memorize exactly what all units did and what the optimal "build order" was or anything.

I'm an intuitive person. I want to be able to improvise and be creative, and I felt like Age of Empires was just making it too complicated and too same-y.

And so, when I wanted to scratch that itch again, I looked for a different modern twist on the idea. Surely new games had come out in the past two decades? Surely some other people had truly evolved the old RTS ideas into greener pastures?

I was disappointed. I could find only _one_ modern game that actually simplified and tried to do something more clever. All the other games were just another RTS with lots of fighting and units and collecting wood and gold. (It's always the fucking wood and gold in medieval times. And archers.)

The one game that seemed interesting was **The Fertile Crescent**. A simple 2D pixel art style, which meant my terrible old laptop could actually run it. Fewer options, no "ages" to go through, main focus on a single resource ("food"), etcetera.

As I played the game, this impression turned out to be completely correct. I liked the game a lot. In fact, the game surprised me with a few more design decisions that I think are genius and absolutely what this genre needs. 

Let's go into some details. 

{{% remark %}}
Note that I played the early access version of TFC2. But from what I can gather, the style and gameplay is very similar to the first instalment.
{{% /remark %}}

First design decision: **food is your most important resource, and it's a recurring expense**. This is both completely realistic (food availability has always decided _everything_ in our history; units need to be fed even after making them) and simplifies your focus (you know your first priority, at all times).

The first thing you check is if food is running low. If so, you drop everything else you were doing, and try to find more food/build farms/reduce your number of units. It gives a simple focus through the overwhelming number of stuff to do in an RTS game, especially late-game. Right from the very first game, without any tutorial, I understood this and could start playing with it.

Second design decision: **villager creation is automatic**. It's like the first rule of Age of Empires that I discovered as a kid (and learned over and over). If your town centre ever stalls or idles, then you're falling behind, which will have devastating effects in the long run. But in AoE, it means constantly checking on your town center and spamming the "Villager" button a random number of times, like being forced to babysit some really unhelpful person. 

In this game, they just spawn automatically, which both removes the unfun parts of AoE _and_ puts a natural loop around your duties as the civilization manager. Do some stuff here, handle that fight over there, OH new villager spawned nice let's assign them to something---repeat. 

I always hated in AoE how you could basically forget all about your old buildings and people and focus entirely on some other area. How the game transitions to just "spam attack and hope you have better numbers" at some point until someone wins. This game keeps asking questions about how best to use your growing number of villagers while constantly pushing up the food requirements, even in the late game when you're moving some massive army into the opponent's base.

{{% remark %}}
After some games I learned you _can_ turn off this automatic production. If a game runs long enough, you probably want to do that. I've read some people hate that villagers spawn automatically, but I don't think their arguments hold any weight. It absolutely improves the game, and as long as there's a toggle to turn it off if s desired, then what's the issue?
{{% /remark %}}

Third design decision: **no ages, fewer buildings, fewer units**. I think it's still trying to do too much here, but it's fine.

What do I mean by this? Or, rather, why do I _like_ having fewer options? Well, RTS games are about managing civilizations. This means it's mostly about macromanagement, the big strokes, instead of controlling one specific character or a hero or whatever. 

So, through the lens of "decide what should happen in big strokes", it just never made sense to me that you would have three "archer"-type units with minor differences. That you would control it down to the single unit, of a single exact type. 

My mind prefers thinking in those big strokes: "we need units that SHOOT FROM DISTANCE behind the walls" What exact units? I don't care! It's not fun to read the exact numbers and descriptions and figure it out! I just need a block of units that shoots from distance, any will do.

Similarly, I might be "we need units that BREAK DOWN WALLS/BUILDINGS" What exact units? I don't care! I don't need choices here. I don't want to memorize a very specific upgrade that would make them stronger. Just give me one "BREAK DOWN WALLS UNITS STRONGER NOW" upgrade to rule them all.

In TFC2, the late-stage categories have only a _single_ unit in them. (Early stages, like archers and infantry, have three options, but only once you have a few upgrades.) This is not a bug, this is a massive feature to me.

Again, it's all down to the type of game you're playing, the genre. If I were to play a "micromanagement" game, such as a first person shooter, _then_ it makes sense to be extremely precise about your exact weapons/skills/upgrades/loadout. But in a game with multiple civilizations, on a big map, zoomed out, large scale, I _don't_ want that. I want the big strokes, and this game does a great job in simplifying the RTS (or AoE) experience in that direction.

Another way that TFC2 does this is by automatically upgrading all your existing units. When you purchase some better version technology of them, you _don't_ need to create all these new units now. Your old ones are just immediately the new ones. When I realized this, in my first game, it felt much more satisfying than upgrading units in AoE.

Is it perfect? No. The tech tree is a bit odd. It's not a tree, it's just a small list of technologies in no logical order (that I can recognize). Even after picking your civilization, the options are pretty identical between them, so I failed to see the point. The "Furnace" gives you a bunch of upgrades for units, which are again too numerous to _really_ understand each one, so you're mostly clicking randomly. 

I think a better simplification here would be to just have a few major categories of upgrades, which decide the main "direction" or "momentum" of your civ. ("Will we be strong on the water? Will we focus on economy? Will we be aggressive or defensive?") Alternatively, you could put upgrades on individual buildings, so you might upgrade the "Barracks" to make all units build in it stronger henceforth.

Fourth design decision: **fertile soil, or short-term versus long-term**. The entire economy revolves around farms and clay pits only working when placed on specific soil. This means that every game you need to improvise based on the randomly generated map you discover. Scouting becomes extra important. Ideal placement becomes extra important. Those first few decisions feel way more impactful while allowing way more freedom.

At the same time, your buildings _change the soil_ over time. A clay pit, once depleted, becomes soil for farms. Trees need to be cut down to make space for farms. This means you are constantly having to make tough decisions that will only pay out long-term. That is _interesting_. That demands some _creativity_ and varied gameplay.

{{% remark %}}
My only gripe here is the sometimes extreme scarcity of metal. If you have bad luck with your spawn, there's just nothing you can do---you will be without much metal for the whole game. So you can't make the stronger late-game units, which means the game has to end within 30 minutes otherwise you will lose.
{{% /remark %}}

I wish the game had leaned into this even more. Allow people to cut rivers/expand water, some irrigation thing maybe. Allow workers to cut through hills/mountains to open up new pathways. The randomly generated terrain you have to work with is one of the most interesting and powerful elements of RTS games ... and yet they rarely use it.

More specifically, it means playing the game more often makes you better _in the right ways_. You learn how to manipulate the systems more intuitively---as opposed to memorizing hotkeys, build orders, the quirks of the game to exploit, etcetera. 

It is my belief, backed up by more and more research, that fun comes from _learning_. Or, rather, a _good learning environment_. For a game to keep my interest, it has to teach me something every time I play.

* Game 1: Ah, you can put multiple people on one Clay Pit. That's why I was out of wood all the time. Ah, the Resource Camp does _not_ collect Food, you need a Granary. That's why I was out of food all the time.
* Game 2: Ah, I should never hit population cap, that's why I fell behind in economy; I should focus on houses earlier. Ah, I should not be building farms in the early stages, hunting/berries are way better at that moment.
* Game 3: Ah, I should assign villagers specifically to swatches of trees that need to be _gone_ in 10 minutes so I can place farms there. Ah, I should place my clay pit there so it can become a farm later too. Ah, I should place some advanced watchtowers to secure the rare metal deposits early---fighting for them later is too costly.
* Game 4: Ah, I can put units _on_ the watchtower. If I place one right, I can both see and defend a pretty good range around my base. This suggests rushing to get 4 archers very early in the game, but only if I have a good watchtower spot. Otherwise I should wall it in; with an extra clay pit, walls are quite cheap and quick.
  * By this point, I had comfortably beat the first two difficulty levels of computer opponents (Casual and Normal, I believe). As in, I played it safe and wasn't trying hard, and my economy and army were just always twice the size of everyone else.

This is what you want. You can start your first game without any tutorial or introduction really. But with every game you play, you can _clearly_ see where you went wrong, learn, adapt, and see better results next time. I stopped tracking it here, but I do remember already having a plan for what to improve in Game 5.

This is, by the way, also my personal issue with gaming. I am so focused on experimenting, getting better, learning, that I improve quite rapidly in most games. And yes, that's an issue, because it means you also rapidly hit that ceiling where games are boring on easier difficulties ... but to beat really tough opponents, you're gonna have to do homework and take it very seriously. And I don't have time or energy for _that_!

Perhaps my experience with AoE (and similar games) helped out here. But if you go from "I have no clue what this game even is" to "I can almost beat the hardest difficulty" within four games (and no tutorial or training), then a game just won't have a lot of longevity.

Which brings me to my admittedly small number of negative thoughts on this game. They simplified it in _good_ ways, but also some _bad_ ways. Ways that remove depth and make the game more superficial or repetitive. I've been having fun, but I can easily see myself losing interest after having played "just" 10 hours or so. I just don't see enough depth here to lose hundreds of hours in this game.

Besides that, some areas are just a bit clunky. Pathfinding (or general decision making) was baffling a lot of the time. Now, this is _fine_ only because the game is _small_, so I could micromanage and course correct all the time. But it's quite idiotic if I tell an army to attack a building, and half of them just decide to stand still after one hit and the other half attack a random villager that happened to walk by instead.

Sometimes I really struggled to select units as it would keep selecting the _land/terrain_ below them instead. I have never felt the need to do that, so it should probably prefer selecting units over terrain basically all the time.

But these are minor things really. Any game will have them and they don't ruin anything.

So ... what's my takeaway?

My takeaway is **why on earth isn't anyone else evolving the RTS genre and simplifying in the right ways!?** The Fertile Crescent has seen loads of success here. It's made by a pretty small team and is getting a sequel only a few years later. There's clearly an audience for it, and I am one of them. But it still doesn't go all the way and I feel like a _lot_ is left on the table.

After playing those first four matches, I wrote down a massive braindump of ways to do it. Once you start thinking about it, you can see a million different ways that RTS games could have evolved and improved by now. Many problems are just carried over from game to game, doing it this way because Age of Empires did it long ago, and it baffles me. Things could be so much simpler, better, more unique.

Why are these games not incorporating day/night cycles? It's a natural way to put constraints on when it's the best time to scout/attack/move.

Why are these games stuck on the same set of 3--5 resources and the exact same ways to collect them?

Why do they not use the randomly generated environment for more things, including terraforming, long-term planning, picking smart spots for attacks/sieges, etcetera?

Why are they all stuck in the same time period with the same units?

But nobody is really doing it. Maybe _I_ have to do it ...

It's a good game. I think you'll have fun intuitively learning the systems for at least 5 or 10 games. I haven't experienced much of the campaign/story, so I can't say much about it.

Those were my thoughts for today.
