_In an attempt to play more games as a game developer and learn from them, I've started trying out all sorts of games. This week I've tried "Let's Build A Zoo". Below are my quick thoughts on it!_

As a kid, I played a ton of Rollercoaster Tycoon and Zoo Tycoon. When I look for a new game to play, it's basically my first instinct to open those games again. But I've played them to death already, and they're really showing their age now, so I haven't played them in a long time.

Instead, I try to find some modern version of those games to try out. 

This will quickly lead you to "Planet Zoo" (and, soon enough, "Planet Zoo 2"). Zoo Tycoon already had quite a steep learning curve; but Planet Zoo is absolutely ridiculous in how many menus and words and systems you need to learn just to play your first game. It's just too overwhelming and not fun at all to me.

And so, after some more searching, I found the ideal candidate: **Let's Build A Zoo**. A simplified pixel art zoo building game by a small team. It looked good, it fit my criteria, so I did not really research it further and just decided to play. (Like any proper gamer, or person with ADHD, I don't do reading or tutorials and just dive in and figure it out :p)

Here we get our first massive positive point: this game does a great job **teaching itself naturally**. You start with an empty zoo and only able to do one or two pretty obvious things. As you grow, new tasks or technology appear that naturally unlock the next systems. By the time you have to learn some new system of the game, you're ready for it, and have fully understood the _last_ system it was trying to teach.

This is, in my opinion, how any game should do it. In all my life I have never experienced a game with an actual "tutorial" that worked. Dialogs, text, tasks like "press button X now!", more tutorial UI panels ... being forced to read all that takes away the fun so much that I don't really want to play the game anymore. Even worse, it does not actually _teach_ the game. All games teach themselves by _playing_ them, both video games and board games.

Zoo Tycoon does not have a technology tree or progressive system like this. This game does. I was initially surprised by that, but very quickly realized this was a genius move, and it made me excited to play more.

At the start you can only get two types of animals, build only two types of exhibits, place only a few types of path of decoration. This ensures the start of the game is not overwhelming. But by playing you _unlock_ basically anything in the game: shops, paths, amenities, animals, etcetera. Because you can _choose_ your own path through this technology tree, you're basically deciding the difficulty/tutorial for yourself, which fixes all issues one might have.

In fact, I was disappointed this wasn't taken even further. As I played, I felt like there was an even _stronger_ version of this idea, where absolutely _everything_ had to be progressively unlocked this way. It would create a very streamlined tycoon/management game that needs no tutorial and differs from all the others.

Another big positive was the **focus on the mechanics and morality behind zoos**. Zoos exist, mostly, as a way to fund wildlife preservation programs (and research and similar things). People seem to believe that zoos just kidnap random animals from a jungle. No, they rescue endangered or wounded animals, and try to nurse them back to health and give them a home. Most animals are _traded_ between zoos---if your zoo has had giraffes for ten years, let's now put some other animal there. When they can't give them a proper home, they're released back into the wild.

This game models all these mechanics in a very simple and easy-to-use way. I hope it showed some players how zoos actually work, and it does so without being condescending or too political with it (which many people _really_ don't like). It's also simply more _interesting_ to play like that. You're limited by the specific animals being offered or traded, you can't just find/buy/get whatever animal you fancy right now. Restrictions like that create more interesting gameplay.

In my experience, it's a tad _too_ limiting, as I was often frustrated that I literally could not get a different animal or perform any trades for many many days in a row. At some point, your massive exhibit with way too many rabbits becomes boring and does not attract more visitors anymore :p But because many "trades" want animals you simply don't have, you literally can't do anything about it.

There's also just a basic morality system. The game regularly gives you a choice, and the "immoral" choice might lead to lots of money or reputation or whatever, but the "moral" choice allows you to unlock better technologies over time (and perhaps make you feel better about yourself).

Now let's get into some negative points. I think I can summarize them all as **progress is too slow for what little you get in return**. The technology tree is absolutely massive, but that's not really a problem. The problem is that it technology points accumulate very slowly, and the only upgrade that allows multiple researchers is all the way at the end of the tree (and thus impossible to access until you've played for a loooong time). When it takes a few days to get the next upgrade, but half the upgrades are just "you have a new type of shop" or "you can place three new types of trees now" ... it's just not enough of a reward. 

It doesn't change the gameplay enough from day to day. After a while, after the early stage of the game, it felt like I was just doing the exact same things over and over every day. I was making money hand over fist, unlocking as rapidly as I could, but it took far too long to get meaningful gameplay changes. 

For example, at some point you can unlock _Farming_. That _is_ a big change! Once unlocked, an entire new system opens up to you, making your zoo management feel fresh again. You can grow your own food for your animals instead of buying it. However, it takes too long to get there. And once there the required buildings are pretty expensive, so even if I "rushed" towards the technology, I still had to wait many days before I could actually _use_ it.

And so, in all my playing, all my zoos never really got through that middle stage. The gameplay just grew stale and progress came too slowly. I was like "Why would I continue? Just so I can get a slightly different variant of a pig tomorrow? To add to my massive exhibit with all the other pigs? Just to get even more rich, without a fun way to really spend that money?"

That's why I remarked, near the start of this article, that I wanted an even bigger and more impactful technology tree. To make _everything_ in the game hinge on that, which would necessitate accumulating points to spend much more quickly too. Ideally, you would pace it such that you unlock a new big system ("change/addition to the core gameplay") whenever you've been doing the same thing over and over for a few in-game days.

Sure, there were other minor grievances. It takes a bit too much work to stop animals from procreating (and I only discovered that option myself after a while when my rabbit population got out of control :p). Using the nursery to get specific new variants of animals is too flimsy and random---it often warned me that one of the animals was already pregnant, and just letting them get a baby worked far better. I don't see the point in mashing animals together. It could have been a really fun gimmick and introduced some evolutionary or biology elements into the game, but as it stands it's just a slightly different sprite for slightly more visitor money.

But the slow technology progress and zoos becoming uninteresting after the initial stages is the big one.

Now let's end on my final positive thought. And it's the biggest one, the most instructive one, to me as a game developer. **This game shows how you can punch above your weight as a small team of developers. Make clever decisions and you can create a pretty large/deep tycoon game.**

What do I mean by that? I'll give some examples.

**The animals have no animations.** They're just static sprites. You can program a little "squash-and-stretch" (or "bounce") quite easily to get some cute-looking movement. This saves an incredible amount of money and work, especially for a game with hundreds of animals. If I were to make a large game with lots of different characters/entities on my own, I would absolutely do this too. It's just smart. It's just not _necessary_ to draw a realistic walking animation for all animals to get a lively and fun game to play.

**You can't save the game.** It auto-saves whenever a new day starts, and that's it. Once I realized that, I was pretty flabbergasted, and thought surely I was missing an obvious button somewhere. But no, you just can't manually save the game.

Why? Well, at the end of the day, your zoo is empty and static! This is much _easier_ to save. No need to track everyone in the zoo, where they are, what they're doing, etcetera. No need to save things that are "in progress" right now. The devs probably just decided not to bother with a more complicated save system and spend their time elsewhere. Which is _smart_! I am slightly annoyed that I can't just quit the game whenever I want, but I can still recognize this as a clever decision that saved a ton of resources.

Because it's _fine_. You can just fast-forward, end the day, then quit. It's no big deal. 

**The pathfinding is very rudimentary.** I noticed something interesting. I had a nice network of stone paths. Every single exhibit/space/shop in my zoo could be visited by walking over a few straight lines of stone. And yet ... many visitors decided to walk across the sand. A few spots here and there where I had planned to place trees/benches/a shop/whatever, but hadn't gotten around to it yet. Loads of them walked over it.

I started tracking them. And I realized the visitors were walking around nearly randomly. No complex behavior. Just walk, check surroundings (e.g. "shop here? buy something!"), pick a new direction to walk. If that meant they circled the same exhibit over and over, if that meant they missed the paths half the time, so be it.

At some point 95% of my visitors was stuck in this one corner of the zoo, completely ignoring the other half even exists. That's the "downside" of lots of randomness and "dumb" computer behavior. But it's fine! _Most_ of the time, visitors were spread out nicely _as if_ they were very intelligent, and that's enough to make the game fun and functional.

This, again, saves a _lot_ of resources during development. And it's absolutely _fine_ while playing. You're encouraged to build nice paths anyway: they make visitors walk faster and you just need to be smart and block them from entering the wrong areas. That is enough for good gameplay. So the devs rightly figured out that some ingenious pathfinding system would not be necessary.

**Organic systems beat complicated UI.** Because _everything_ in the game follows the technology tree _or_ the build menu, nothing else, it massively reduces the amount of UI you need in the game. The amount of complicated systems you need, which need to be localized too, and respond to different screen sizes, and so forth. 

If I were to make a Zoo Tycoon-esque game, I would do this too (and, as stated, go even further). I would start with the restriction of having as few buttons, panels, walls of text as possible. And I would find a way to do everything within those constraints. It saves development time/effort, but it also saves effort on the side of the player.

This would bypass the need to build some pathing system at all! While also creating an interesting restriction you must work around. I might actually make a prototype for this now ...

So, all together, what do I think?

It's a fun little game to play for a few hours. To start a new zoo, get some things going, get some new animals, build your first subway or farm to make it more efficient. But anything beyond that happens too slowly and does not introduce enough new mechanics (quickly) to keep my interest.

It simplifies the gameplay very well, in the right ways, _most of the time_. I would rather start a new game of this than the more involved Zoo Tycoon/Planet Zoo. At the same time, it misses some quality of life features that would _really_ make it more "cozy" or "smooth". It fails to really make me feel like every animal in my zoo _matters_. That I really want to treat them well, need to preserve each and every one, present them as well as possible to attract visitors. And when that feeling is missing, well, then the fantasy of running a zoo kind of falls apart.

I don't really do grades or final verdicts anymore for games/movies/books.

These were my thoughts, hopefully they were interesting!
