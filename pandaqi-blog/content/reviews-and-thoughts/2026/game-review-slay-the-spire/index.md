_In an attempt to play more games as a game developer and learn from them, I've started trying out all sorts of games. This week I've tried "Slay The Spire (2)". Below are my quick thoughts on it!_

Slay The Spire is a game that I've obviously heard about a gazillion times over the years, and always in a positive way. Whenever I looked at it, though, I saw the same fantasy-monster-esque-card-game as so many other video games. I did not immediately see why it would be so amazing or unique. Though it still had elements that stood out to me as fun or interesting, such as a slightly more colorful and clear visual style than other games, and the type of deck building that I know and love from board games.

I _wanted_ to try it, but it wasn't high priority.

Well, the sequel recently entered Early Access, reminding me of this game and giving me the perfect excuse to finally try it! 

I will write _Slay the Spire_, or _Slay the Spire (2)_ in this review, because the core of both games is the same and I feel these thoughts apply to both. Sure, the sequel has lots of additions and changes, but they're more on the fringes for a new player and not that relevant here. Just for the record, though, I only played the sequel.

{{% remark %}}
As always, I write these thoughts as both a game designer and an experienced board gamer. As I try more games, and write more of these reviews, I've come to realize that my perspective is _not_ the same as a new player looking for a video game. Or really any other gamer at all :p 

I am in this weird situation in which I am immediately somewhat skilled in any game I try, but at the same time I haven't actually played many different _video games_ in my life. I never get _very_ skilled in any game. I immediately take apart game mechanics as I see them and learn to manipulate them, but this also means my focus isn't on winning as much as, you know, a typical gamer.
{{% /remark %}}

## A First Run

It was very easy to start the game and immediately start playing. There are only 3 "tutorial screens" shown when you start your first game that explain the core of the game. 

Everything else happens through cards, artefacts, bonuses that you acquire along the way. Which means they explain themselves _as they enter play_, which is always nice. Less to explain/remember beforehand!

It does mean that you need to actively remind yourself to hover over icons and watch for new icons. The few times I _forgot_ to do this, or what an ability did exactly, were exactly why my very first run ended. That's to be expected---beginner ignorance makes it impossible to _really_ make smart decisions in your first game---though doesn't ever make it less annoying.

The core loop of the game is very strong. 

> "Take next step on map -> Defeat monsters there -> Earn money and be allowed to pick a new card for your deck"

Before long, just through playing, I knew _enough_ about the game to make smarter choices here and start planning a bit more long-term. This is always more powerful than a tutorial. 

{{% remark %}}
In fact, I don't even think those 3 tutorial screens at the start were really necessary. They could have pointed at things as you played or made some even simpler first demo match. But that's nitpicking, and I'm not sure if it would be an improvement, because gamers are notorious for missing things unless you make it blindingly obvious and shove it in your face. Like a big HEY A TUTORIAL SCREEN HERE! instead of some more subtle arrow to a relevant button.
{{% /remark %}}

Each individual battle is short and contained enough that it never drags or feels overwhelming. When chained together, time flies, your deck grows in satisfying ways, and there's never a dull moment or a sudden difficulty spike.

That first run ended with the boss of Act 2 (they're called acts, right? Stages? Levels of the map?) I had to stop playing rapidly, so I chose a risky option to try and beat it _now_, then save and quit. Alas, I am _never_ lucky in games, so I lost in the round before I would have won :p That said, the loss still showed me some ways in which I was going wrong.

I had the sandwormy boss that just defeats you (no matter your health and all) in 4 turns. That showed me that I should've upgraded a few cards, because I just wasn't doing enough raw damage. But, well, I only learnt about "enhancing/upgrading cards" fifteen minutes earlier.

Why am I saying this? Because over the years, as I played and designed more games and studied human psychology, I've come to realize that "FUN = LEARNING". A good learning environment means a fun game. And so I started judging games by how easily I was able to see what I did wrong, adapt, and try again with the lessons learned.

Does Slay the Spire (2) pass that test? Yes it does!

* Your card choices obviously give immediate feedback. Even the more passive ones, like defense, give you feedback on their effectiveness as soon as the _monster_ attacks.
* The map has these "Rest" spots that literally allow you to take a breather and reflect. (Reflection is pretty necessary for learning.) The other special spots on the map also do this in their own way. For example, you'll only run into the shop once in a while. But when you do, and see new cards on offer, you get this chance to reflect on your deck and what did and did not work in recent battles.
* The game tries its best to give feedback about what's going to happen, what's happening, and why it happened. It's not always effective, but most of the time it is. Being able to see what your opponent is _going to do_ has the exciting effect of supercharging that "learning". 
  * The most prominent theory on how people learn is that we "predict an outcome" and then "test what actually happened against the prediction". This is way stronger and more effective than if you only did the last part! (That last part can also be phrased as just "absorbing information" or "seeing what's happening". Without the prediction step before it, learning does not really happen.)
  * And, well, Slay the Spire does the first part! Because you know exactly what each monster will do, you actually get the "predict the future" step. (And then, you learn a lot if the future that ends up happening did not match your earlier prediction.)

As you can see, my favorite (or most "instructive") part of the game is easily the ability to know exactly what your opponents will do. It feels overpowered, right? It feels like it should make the game too easy, or too linear, or too boring. That's why almost no other game does it, right?

Instead, it is, to me, what makes the game tick. 

And I should probably consider it myself when designing my own games. Just give the player all the information, don't hide behind randomness or obscurity or "you have to prepare for ANYTHING the opponent can do!" No, just tell them everything, give them the future, and then _still_ make the game interesting and challenging. Because if you can do that, it makes for a better learning environment (that probably also feels more fair), which leads to more fun.

The biggest reason why I lost that first run was _not looking at what the opponent was doing_. Correcting that, taking into account if the monster was actually going to attack or not _every time_, was a rewarding big improvement to my next run. And that's the thing that makes games fun :)

## Thoughts After Some More Games

I really enjoyed that first run, but it took some time before I could play again. (I was busy running my online store, prototyping game ideas, basically working 24/7.) It didn't feel fair to post a review after just one run that took ~1 hour if I remember correctly. But by now so much time had passed that my second run could just as well have been my first ever run :p

So I tried to play at least a handful more runs before continuing and finishing this review. (Or, well, more just "my thoughts on this game".)

At time of writing, I've played ~10 runs with different characters. 

I was positively surprised by the fact that the different characters actually have some completely unique gameplay element. It's not just a few number tweaks ("oh this one has more health, oh this one starts with 2 extra cards"). Each plays fundamentally differently than the others, showing me new parts of the game. That was nice. Also, it gave me some ideas for deck-building games of my own, which I wrote down and might try to tackle one day.

Of course, the downside of trying every new thing as it unlocked, was that I only got in one or two runs for each character. Not enough to really get good at _that particular character_ (or even see most of what it can do).

So I stopped switching characters and stuck with _Ironclad_ for a while. And I somehow got ... worse? :/

My first handful of runs in this game all ended with an incredibly narrow defeat at the hands of the Act 2 Boss. It was actually getting a bit ridiculous how similar they were. If I had _one more turn_, if I had _one little bit of luck_, I would have beaten that Act 2 boss and gotten into Act 3. 

This "success" was a combination of following my intuition from earlier (deck-building) games, and learning the cards and details of Slay the Spire so I wasn't making obvious mistakes. I was quite naively grabbing every good card I came across. I alternated between buying something offensive and buying some block, and it took four runs before I even realized you could buy relics and potions in the store :p

But once I started actually applying strategies, experimenting, going for specific builds, skipping cards to not bloat my deck, I started failing runs much earlier than before. Maybe it's simply because I stopped playing safely. Maybe it's because I was comfortable with the game and losing runs in spectacular(ly dumb) fashion. But I stopped being able to even reach the Act 2 Boss or many runs in a row, even though I was _actually_ going for synergies and smarter options now. 

I created a deck that kept spamming Vulnerable onto enemies, and then had a few cards that turned the Vulnerable into either extra damage, block, or Strength for me. This was extremely powerful and it felt like I was onto something, but then I just kind of died halfway through Act 2 with absolutely no way to save myself?

I created a deck that, instead, focused on all sorts of abilities that triggered when playing Attack cards. So I could have loads of cheap Attack cards, which made other cards cheaper/more powerful, which allowed me to play even more cards, and thus my attack was rather strong (without having to buy expensive/stronger cards per se). Again, I thought I was onto something! Easily winning most battles, then suddenly I had the spectacular luck of not drawing _any_ of my block cards for three turns in a row while the enemy decided to do some insane attacks three turns in a row.

I hate to say it, but this is an example of why I usually don't play games with lots of randomness :p I _always_ have bad luck. This is not an exaggeration. I am _incredibly_ unlucky in games, consistently, always. I've played many games of Risk where I just rolled 1s and 2s fifty times in a row and nothing else. I've played games of Exploding Kittens where a single card immediately eliminated me at the start of the game because of how unlucky I am. I have never played a single Monopoly game where I did not land in jail every time it was even _remotely_ possible to do so.

When I analyzed what went wrong in those failed runs, "unlucky" was all I could say. I had made good choices. I was easily winning all these battles. I'd have to be _very_ nitpicky to find a card that should not be in my deck or cards that I should've played in a different order or something. I replayed that fatal battle in my head, and simply could not find anything I should've done differently. The _only_ thing I could've done was what made my first runs so succesful: be very defensive and careful and basically _assume_ you'll always have the worst of luck and get the right cards for that. But that is both a boring way to play and not enough to progress into Act 3 (because you're just doing way too little damage and moving too slowly).

This is a strike _against_ the game being a "good learning environment", which reduces some of the fun. But ... it's really a personal thing. I can't overstate how incredibly unlucky I am in all games of all kinds. If you had seen me die on those final runs, you would agree with me :p 

{{% example %}}
All attacks being 1 or 2 HP off from _killing_ enemies. Drawing precisely the wrong cards every single time. Having all my block cards when nobody is attacking, having none when everyone is suddenly attacking. Being 1 card or 1 energy short of getting any synergy or combo three turns in a row. The enemy applying exactly the one debuff that I haven't been able to buy a countercard for. All of that and more, every dang time. This is never changing, I know that by now :p
{{% /example %}}

I guess the better question is: _Is the game good/fun enough for me to keep playing and wanting to improve DESPITE the randomness and bad luck?_

And ... yeah, I think it is. I've played this game more than any of the others I've tried, or at least for longer (and more repeated attempts after failure). Instead of thinking about how annoying my bad luck is, I keep thinking that _maybe_ next time I'll find a way to counteract it and get further. That's the mark of a challenging game that you still want to keep playing.

## Conclusion

I decided to finish this review and post it after those initial 15 runs or so. No use delaying it any further. I think I've experienced a large part of the game by now and certainly put in the hours to form an opinion.

As it stands, I have not beaten the game yet. But I'll keep trying once in a while---which I can't say for any of the other games I've reviewed so far. That's a big compliment to Slay the Spire (2) and probably the best summary of my thoughts/this review.
